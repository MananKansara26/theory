=> How do you handle conflict with a client or project manager?
Best structure to remember: Understand → Explain impact → Offer solution → Align → Document

Example 1 : HealthRay — Hospital wants customized functionality (Best answer)

Situation
A hospital wanted functionality different from the standard production behavior.
The hospital was an important/high-value customer.
Business decided to create a separate branch/custom implementation.

Your concern
Separate organization-specific branch means:
- code duplication
- difficult bug fixes
- difficult deployments
- future merges become complicated
- maintenance cost increases
More customers could potentially ask for similar customizations.

Action
Instead of simply rejecting the idea:
Explained the long-term maintenance risk.
Suggested making the functionality configurable.
Example:
- organization-level configuration
- feature flag
- tenant-specific settings
- permission/configuration-based behavior
Keep one codebase.
Enable/disable the behavior based on organization configuration.

Result
Customer gets the required functionality.
No separate codebase/branch needed.
Future changes remain easier to maintain.
Other customers can potentially use the same capability through configuration.

"I didn't want to choose between business requirements and engineering quality. I tried to find a solution where we could satisfy the customer while keeping a single maintainable codebase."

Example 2 — Client changing requirements
Don't say "it was due to the requirement increment" in a way that sounds like you're blaming the client.

Situation: Client gave a requirement with a fixed deadline.
Started development based on the initial scope.
During development, client kept adding/changing requirements.
Action: I didn't directly reject the changes.
Explained how the additional requirements would affect the timeline and existing scope.
Discussed which requirements were critical for the current release.
Suggested either:
1.prioritize the new requirements, or
2.move them to the next release.
Kept the client/project manager updated about the revised timeline.
Result: We aligned on priorities instead of compromising the quality.
Learning: Requirement changes are normal, but they need scope and timeline management.

I don't treat conflict as an argument. I first try to understand the reason behind the request, explain the technical or timeline impact, and then propose options.

Example 3 - Conflict with Project Manager — RequestWeed
But be careful with saying: "I told my manager I need to write maintainable code." That can sound like you're prioritizing engineering preferences over business deadlines.

Situation: RequestWeed was in the initial development stage.
Manager wanted the feature completed quickly because of the deadline.
I understood the urgency and business requirement.
Concern: The initial architecture/code structure would become the foundation for future features.
If we rushed the foundation:
1. more technical debt
2. harder maintenance
3. slower future development
Action: Explained the trade-off to the manager.
Proposed spending some additional time on the initial setup/architecture.
Once the foundation was ready, feature development would become much faster.
Focused on minimum necessary structure, not over-engineering.
Result: We maintained a good foundation while continuing development toward the deadline.

I wasn't trying to delay the feature; I was trying to avoid creating technical debt at the foundation of the project.



=> When did you demonstrate leadership skills?

Leadership ≠ being a manager.
For a 4-year developer, leadership can mean:
identifying problems proactively
taking ownership
helping teammates
improving processes
finding cost-saving solutions
taking initiative without being asked

Example 1 : Frigid AI / MongoDB optimization

Situation: While working on Frigid AI, I noticed some MongoDB aggregation queries were taking too long.

I had previous experience working heavily with MongoDB aggregations.
Identified potential optimization opportunities.
At that time, I had an urgent assigned task, so I didn't abandon my priority task.
I noted the potential improvements.
Once my assigned work was completed and I had some capacity:
- discussed it with the manager
- prioritized the issues
- optimized the queries one by one

Result:
improved query performance
reduced unnecessary database processing
received positive feedback from the client/team.
Leadership point: I didn't wait for someone to assign the problem to me.

Strong opening: "For me, leadership is also about identifying problems outside my immediate task and taking ownership of them when the priority allows."

Example 2 : RequestWeed — Saving client's money

Situation: Client was paying for a third-party scraping service.
While working/checking the website, I noticed the website was exposing the required data through a public API.
Instead of continuing with the paid scraping approach:
- investigated the API
- tested it using Postman
- understood the required request/response structure
Within roughly half a day, created a small solution using the API.
Demonstrated it to the client.
Client was happy.
Result: Reduced dependency on the paid scraping service and helped save the client money.
Leadership: Proactively looked beyond my assigned implementation and found a better solution.



=> Tell me about a time you experienced failure

Example 1 — Git/server mistake

Situation:
Early in my career, I had limited experience with Git/server management.
There were some local changes on the server.
I had an urgent deployment to perform.
While pulling the latest changes, Git indicated that local changes were preventing the pull.

Mistake:
I searched for a solution online.
Found a command that would reset the local changes.
I used it without fully understanding its impact.
The existing server-side changes were lost.

Action:
I immediately understood that I had made a mistake.
Focused on restoring the required state and completing the deployment safely.
After that incident, I changed how I worked with production servers.

Learning:
Never run a command in production just because it appears in Stack Overflow.
Understand the command and its impact first.
Follow proper Git/version-control practices.
Avoid making manual/untracked changes on servers.
Use proper deployment/version-control processes.

"It was an early-career mistake, but it changed my approach to production work. Today, I don't blindly execute commands in production; I first understand the impact and use a controlled deployment process."

Example 2 : AI-generated code failure
AI-assisted development → insufficient review → missed edge cases → improved process

Situation: I started using AI tools to speed up development.

Initially, I trusted some generated code more than I should have.
I didn't review every part of the generated logic deeply enough.
During testing/review, some edge cases were discovered.
Action:
- fixed the issues
- added more comprehensive test cases
- reviewed the generated code more carefully
- validated business logic independently
Learning:
AI is a productivity tool, not a replacement for engineering judgment.
Developer is still responsible for:
- architecture
- correctness
- security
- edge cases
- testing
Current approach: I use AI to accelerate implementation, but I review and validate the output before considering it production-ready.

"The main lesson for me was that faster code generation doesn't necessarily mean faster delivery. Correctness and validation are still my responsibility."


=> How did you handle the conflict with the team member?
understand → discuss → explain trade-off → try to align → escalate only when necessary → maintain relationship.

Situation:
While working at Healthray, I had a disagreement with a frontend developer.
He wanted to reuse the existing listing API and return all the additional data needed for the page.
My concern was that the listing API was already costly/heavy.

Understand:
First, I understood his concern.
Reusing the existing API would mean:
less frontend/backend changes
faster implementation
simpler flow from his perspective.

My concern:
Adding more data to the existing listing API would make an already expensive API even heavier.
It could increase:
response time
database/query cost
unnecessary data transfer
impact on other users/features using the same API.

Action:
I explained these consequences to him rather than simply saying his approach was wrong.
Suggested creating a separate API specifically for the additional data.
We discussed the trade-off.
He still preferred the existing approach.
Since we couldn't reach an agreement and it could impact the system, I discussed the technical trade-off with the manager.

Result:
Manager agreed that separating the API was the better approach.
We proceeded with the more appropriate solution.
I maintained a good working relationship with the teammate; the disagreement was about the technical approach, not the person.

Learning:
Technical disagreements are normal in a team.
First try to resolve them directly with the person.
Use technical reasoning/data, not personal opinions.
Escalate only when the disagreement can materially affect the project/system.
Strong opening pattern

"I believe conflict with a team member should be handled through technical discussion rather than making it personal. I first try to understand their reasoning, explain my concern with the impact, and find a solution we both agree on."

=> How would you handle a delay in your task?
Identify early → Communicate → Prioritize → Solve → Support
"The biggest mistake is communicating a delay only when the deadline has already arrived."

First, I try to identify potential blockers as early as possible.
If I realize the deadline may be affected, I communicate it to the manager/stakeholder early rather than waiting until the deadline.
Explain:
- what is blocking me
- expected impact
- what I have already tried
- what support I need
Re-prioritize tasks based on business impact.
If possible, break the task into smaller deliverables.
Put extra effort/time when necessary to recover the delay.
If another team member has a similar blocker and my priority work is completed, I help them as well.
Goal: Don't surprise the team at the last moment.
